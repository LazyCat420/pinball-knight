# Asteroids (1979): Frame Rate by Subtraction — and How to Optimize WebGPU for Pinball Knight

**Why this game is in the research set.** Asteroids is the purest historical example of a game that got its smoothness not by making the hardware faster, but by refusing to do any work that didn't correspond to something the player could see. Its Digital Vector Generator drew *only the lines that existed* — no framebuffer, no raster overdraw, no per-pixel cost for empty space — and its game design (a hard object cap, wraparound play space, precompiled shape subroutines in ROM) was consciously shaped to fit the machine's budget. That is exactly the discipline Pinball Knight needs on WebGPU, where our own investigations already established that frame stalls come from **render pipeline count, not scene size**, that first-use pipeline compilation causes hitches, and that point-light count kills a forward renderer. This report reconstructs how Asteroids hit its frame rate in 1979, extracts the transferable principles, and then maps them onto current WebGPU / Three.js `WebGPURenderer` best practice — ending with a prioritized checklist for this game.

---

## 1. How Asteroids actually achieved its smoothness

### 1.1 The hardware split: a 6502 for the game, a DVG for the drawing

Asteroids ran on two cooperating processors: a single [MOS 6502 executing the game program, and QuadraScan — Atari's high-resolution vector graphics processor, also called the "XY display system" and the Digital Vector Generator (DVG)](https://www.technologyuk.net/computing/computer-gaming/gaming-landmarks-1960-1985/asteroids.shtml). The 6502 by itself was too slow to run the game *and* steer the CRT beam, so beam control was delegated entirely to the DVG, which [received graphics commands from the 6502 and generated the analog deflection signals for the monochrome vector monitor](https://www.technologyuk.net/computing/computer-gaming/gaming-landmarks-1960-1985/asteroids.shtml). The DVG design came out of Atari's Grass Valley research lab in 1978 and was productized by engineer [Howard Delman, who refined it for Lunar Lander (1979) before Asteroids reused it](https://www.jmargolin.com/vgens/vgens.htm).

This is the 1979 version of a modern truth: the CPU that runs your simulation should hand the display a *pre-digested program of work*, not micromanage every dot. In Pinball Knight terms: the 60Hz sim tick produces state; the GPU consumes command buffers built from persistent, precompiled resources.

### 1.2 The vector display: draw only what exists

A vector (XY) monitor has no framebuffer and no scanlines. As Atari engineer Jed Margolin documents in [The Secret Life of Vector Generators](https://www.jmargolin.com/vgens/vgens.htm), the DVG was "a custom processor made out of SSI and MSI" — up/down counters feeding X and Y DACs, with Binary Rate Multipliers clocking the counters so the beam swept *directly from point to point*, tracing each line segment once. There is no per-pixel work, no clearing of a frame, no overdraw of black space. An empty screen costs almost nothing; a busy screen costs exactly the sum of its line segments.

The DVG executed a small display-list instruction set out of vector RAM/ROM: [`VCTR` (draw a vector), `JSRL` (jump to a vector subroutine), `RTSL` (return), and `HALT`](https://www.jmargolin.com/vgens/vgens.htm). The `JSRL` instruction is the key optimization: every asteroid shape, the ship, the saucer, the explosion debris, and the character set were stored **once** as subroutines in 2 KB of vector ROM, and the per-frame display list in RAM was little more than a series of "position the beam here, scale by this, call shape #3" entries. The [main game program fit in 6 KB of ROM, with that 2 KB vector ROM holding the descriptions of all graphical elements](https://www.technologyuk.net/computing/computer-gaming/gaming-landmarks-1960-1985/asteroids.shtml). The full annotated disassembly is public at [6502disassembly.com](https://6502disassembly.com/va-asteroids/) and [Computer Archeology](https://computerarcheology.com/Arcade/Asteroids/), including the DVG display-list documentation.

So the per-frame cost model was:

- **Static shapes:** authored once, "compiled" into vector ROM at build time. Zero per-frame preparation.
- **Per-frame work:** proportional strictly to the number of *live objects*, each contributing a few display-list words plus its line segments.
- **Empty space:** free. No raster scan means the 95% of the screen that is black vacuum consumes no draw time.

### 1.3 The budget, in Ed Logg's own words

Ed Logg (programmer) and Lyle Rains (who conceived the asteroid-shooting premise) built the game deliberately inside this envelope. Logg fought for vector hardware over raster because of precision: ["The most important was the higher resolution, 1024x768 instead of 320x240. I felt we needed the extra resolution to show where the ship was aiming"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/outstanding-ideas-ed-logg-on-asteroids-and-gauntlet). And he was explicit that the frame rate was the fixed point and content flexed around it: ["there were limitations such as the amount of stuff I could draw and still update the game at 60Hz"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/outstanding-ideas-ed-logg-on-asteroids-and-gauntlet).

The object cap was real and enforced in code and by memory: [the game program only allows 26 asteroids on the screen at any one time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids_(video_game)), and Logg confirmed the RAM ceiling directly: ["The limited RAM also limited the number of asteroids I could draw so you may see some large asteroids go away with a single shot if the screen is full"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/outstanding-ideas-ed-logg-on-asteroids-and-gauntlet) — i.e., when the budget was exhausted, a large asteroid that should split into two mediums simply *didn't spawn the children*. Degradation was designed, not accidental.

Two more period details show how tight the coupling between design and hardware was:

- **The spot killer.** The monitor had a protection circuit that shut off the beam if it didn't move enough (to avoid burning the phosphor). Logg had to guarantee ["enough deflection on the screen so the spot killer did not turn off the beam"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/outstanding-ideas-ed-logg-on-asteroids-and-gauntlet) — on some units the screen would dim when only the ship and three small asteroids remained. Even *minimum* scene content was a hardware constraint.
- **The famous trails were free.** The slow phosphor of the B&W monitor left glowing trails behind shots and ships — ["this was not part of any design"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/outstanding-ideas-ed-logg-on-asteroids-and-gauntlet), as Logg admitted; [Arcade Blogger's development history](https://arcadeblogger.com/2018/10/24/atari-asteroids-creating-a-vector-arcade-classic/) tells the same story. The lesson: some of the most-loved "VFX" cost zero cycles because they fell out of the display technology.

### 1.4 Design that matched the budget

The game design is a list of budget-respecting choices:

- **Small entity count by rule.** 26 asteroids max, one player ship, at most one saucer, a handful of bullets. The wave structure ramps difficulty through *speed and splitting*, not raw count.
- **Wraparound space.** The toroidal playfield ([objects leaving one edge reappear on the opposite edge](https://arcadeblogger.com/2018/10/24/atari-asteroids-creating-a-vector-arcade-classic/)) means there is exactly one screen of world: no camera, no scrolling, no culling problem, no off-screen simulation. Everything that exists is visible, so "work proportional to visible entities" and "work proportional to all entities" are the same number.
- **Precomputed shapes, four asteroid outlines.** All variety comes from scale and rotation of a few ROM subroutines — the 1979 equivalent of instancing a shared mesh.
- **Visible overload behavior.** When players exploited the game into 50–100+ spare lives (each drawn on screen), [the game measurably slowed down, and past ~250 lives the watchdog could reset the machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids_(video_game)) — direct proof the frame cost was linear in drawn objects, and that the designers had simply never budgeted for that many.

It worked: Asteroids became Atari's best-selling coin-op, with [about 47,840 upright and 8,725 cocktail cabinets sold — 70,000+ units worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids_(video_game)).

## 2. The principles that translate to 2026

1. **Do work proportional to visible entities, not to world size.** The DVG's cost was the sum of live line segments; black space was free. Modern equivalent: draw calls and vertices for *live, on-screen* objects only — cull aggressively, and make empty dungeon corridors cost nothing (Pinball Knight's fog of war is already halfway to this if it gates rendering, not just visibility).
2. **Precompute everything static, at build/load time.** Vector ROM subroutines = today's precompiled pipelines, baked geometry, and atlased textures. Anything the DVG would have had to "figure out" per frame was instead assembled offline ([even vector brightness normalization was done during program assembly](https://www.jmargolin.com/vgens/vgens.htm)). The frame loop should only *reference* resources, never *create* them.
3. **Cap entity counts by design, and define the degradation.** Asteroids didn't discover its limit in the field; the limit was a game rule (26 asteroids), and the over-budget behavior was chosen (asteroids vanish instead of splitting). A cap you pick is a feature; a cap the GPU picks for you is a stutter.
4. **Split simulation from presentation.** The 6502 ran game logic at a fixed 60Hz while the DVG independently walked the display list and drove the beam. Pinball Knight's fixed 60Hz accumulator timestep is the same architecture — protect it: the sim tick must never block on rendering work (and especially never on pipeline compilation).
5. **Let the display technology give you free aesthetics.** Phosphor trails cost nothing and defined the game's look. Equivalent today: effects that ride existing passes (emissive materials, additive blending on already-drawn quads) instead of effects that add passes, pipelines, or lights.

## 3. Modern WebGPU optimization practice

This section is the applied core: current best practice from the WebGPU ecosystem, mapped to a Three.js `WebGPURenderer` game.

### 3.1 Pipelines are the modern vector ROM: compile them before the game starts

Our own ground truth: **frame stalls track pipeline count, not scene size** — the first frame that *uses* a new render pipeline pays its compilation cost. Industry practice agrees:

- WebGPU exposes [`createRenderPipelineAsync` / `createComputePipelineAsync`, which resolve only when the pipeline is ready to use without stalling](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/) — the synchronous variants can block the queue at first draw. (From [Toji.dev WebGPU Best Practices](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/), maintained by Brandon Jones of the Chrome WebGPU team.)
- Three.js exposes this as [`renderer.compileAsync(scene, camera)`](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/Renderer.html), which walks a scene, builds every material's pipeline, and returns a promise — the supported way to pre-warm shaders during a load screen instead of hitching on first sight of a material. Community threads confirm the failure mode we saw: [shader/pipeline compilation stutter the first time an object with a new shader is rendered](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/webgpurenderer-shader-compilation-time-is-long-with-models-with-a-lot-of-morphs/60446), and there are known sharp edges (e.g. [compileAsync interacting badly with MRT setups, three.js #31220](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/31220)) — so the warm-up scene must exercise the *same render targets* the game uses.
- **Minimize pipeline permutations.** Every distinct material/shader variant × vertex layout × blend state × render-target format is a separate pipeline. Fewer, more general shaders (the "uber-shader" end of the spectrum) trade a little per-pixel branching for far fewer compiles; many specialized variants trade the opposite. For a 2.5D sprite-heavy game, a handful of shared materials is almost always the right end of that spectrum.
- **Own your bind group layouts.** Toji's guidance: [get as many pipelines as possible to re-use the same bind group layouts, group resources by update frequency, and reserve `layout: 'auto'` for one-off pipelines](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/bind-groups.html) — shared layouts let the driver skip rebinding work and make pipelines interchangeable mid-pass. Three.js manages this internally, but the *material* choices you make (same material class, same feature flags) determine whether it can share.
- Be aware of engine-level costs too: `WebGPURenderer` has a documented [per-render-item UBO update cost that grows with the number of distinct render items (three.js #30560)](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/30560) and [known cases where it trails WebGL until draw counts are consolidated (#31055)](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/31055) — one more reason instancing (below) is not optional.

### 3.2 Instancing and batching: the JSRL of the GPU era

Asteroids drew four rock outlines at many scales/rotations via ROM subroutine calls. The modern equivalent:

- [`THREE.InstancedMesh` renders hundreds or thousands of transforms of one geometry+material in a single draw call](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html); Codrops' guide shows [the pattern and its restriction — one geometry, one material per InstancedMesh; three shapes = three draw calls, not thousands](https://tympanus.net/codrops/2025/07/10/three-js-instances-rendering-multiple-objects-simultaneously/). Draw-call reduction of this kind is routinely a [10x+ frame-rate difference on large object counts](https://threejsroadmap.com/blog/draw-calls-the-silent-killer).
- Instancing is not automatically a win: there are [reported cases where InstancedMesh underperforms plain meshes when instance counts are low and per-instance vertex cost is high (three.js #30352)](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/30352). For our case — hundreds of low-poly zombie billboards/meshes, thousands of particle quads — it is squarely in the win column, but measure (§3.6).
- Merge truly static geometry (a floor's walls, rails, and track pieces) into as few buffers/meshes as possible at floor-generation time; [custom merged BufferGeometry is the standard fix for draw-call-bound Three.js scenes](https://57blocks.com/blog/improve-performance-on-threejs-scenes-using-custom-meshes-and-buffergeometry?tab=engineering). A generated maze floor is *static for the whole floor* — it should compile down to a handful of draws, exactly like a vector-ROM level would.

### 3.3 Texture atlases: fewer textures → fewer bind groups → bigger batches

Batching breaks whenever state changes. The 2D-engine world has measured this for years: PixiJS's batcher can [combine sprites into one draw call only while they share a texture set (up to 16 textures per batch, hardware-dependent) and a blend mode — different blend modes break the batch](https://pixijs.com/8.x/guides/concepts/performance-tips). The fix is the classic sprite atlas: pack zombie frames, particle sprites, damage-number glyphs, and tile art into a few large atlas textures so one bind group serves thousands of quads. In WebGPU terms, that directly cuts bind group switches, which is [exactly what Toji's bind-group guidance optimizes for](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/bind-groups.html).

### 3.4 Buffers: never re-create, rarely re-upload

- Do not allocate GPU buffers in the frame loop. Create persistent buffers at load; update the dynamic ones (instance matrices, particle attributes) with `queue.writeBuffer`-style updates of *only the dirty range*.
- Prefer per-instance attributes over per-object uniforms so one upload covers a whole population (this is what `InstancedMesh.instanceMatrix` does under the hood).
- For command-recording overhead specifically, WebGPU offers [render bundles — pre-recorded command sequences that replay with near-zero JS/validation cost, ideal for large sets of draws repeated every frame](https://toji.dev/webgpu-best-practices/render-bundles.html). Three.js does not yet expose bundles for general scenes, but the *shape* of the advice still applies: make the per-frame encoded command list short and stable.

### 3.5 Particles belong on the GPU

CPU particle systems hit a wall long before GPU ones: comparative writeups report [CPU-updated 10k-particle systems costing tens of milliseconds while WebGPU compute updates 100k+ particles in low single-digit milliseconds, because simulation and rendering share GPU-resident buffers with zero per-frame CPU↔GPU transfer](https://markaicode.com/webgpu-physics-simulation-1m-particles/). Hobbyist and academic demos routinely run [hundreds of thousands to millions of compute-simulated particles at 60fps in the browser](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/particle-life-simulation-in-browser-using-webgpu.html) ([TU Wien bachelor's thesis on WebGPU particle systems](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2023/PETER-2023-PSW/PETER-2023-PSW-.pdf)). Three.js's WebGPU node system (TSL) supports compute passes, and [compute-driven galaxy/particle demos are the canonical example](https://threejsroadmap.com/blog/galaxy-simulation-webgpu-compute-shaders). For Pinball Knight: gore bursts, sparks, and dust are *pure visuals* — they never need to touch the 60Hz sim, so they are the ideal candidates to move wholesale into a compute pass that costs one pipeline (pre-warmed) and zero CPU time.

### 3.6 Measuring honestly (the part everyone gets wrong)

Our project already learned two of these the hard way; here is the full protocol the ecosystem recommends:

1. **Verify you are on real hardware first.** Chrome silently hands out software adapters. Check [`adapter.info` — an architecture of `swiftshader` (or a renderer string containing SwiftShader/llvmpipe/"Software" in `about:gpu`) means you are timing a CPU pretending to be a GPU](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/webgpu/troubleshooting-tips), and every number you collect is invalid for GPU-bound questions. This matches our own finding that SwiftShader invalidates GPU timing experiments. (Chrome has been [deprecating the silent SwiftShader-backed fallback](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/yhFguWS_3pM), but headless/WSL environments still land on it.)
2. **CPU-side `performance.now()` around `render()` does not measure the GPU.** WebGPU work is submitted asynchronously; the JS timer only sees encoding cost. Use the [`timestamp-query` feature: the GPU writes its own clock at the start and end of a pass, you resolve the query set into a buffer and read the delta in nanoseconds](https://webgpufundamentals.org/webgpu/lessons/webgpu-timing.html). Note the feature is optional and must be requested at device creation, and [Chrome quantizes results (~100µs granularity depending on isolation) to blunt timing attacks](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/dtYJ0MQYMlU) — fine for "is this pass 0.5ms or 5ms", useless for nanosecond micro-benchmarks. Practical walkthroughs: [Omar Shehata's timestamp-query guide](https://omar-shehata.medium.com/how-to-use-webgpu-timestamp-query-9bf81fb5344a), [profiling WebGPU shaders in Three.js](https://threejsroadmap.com/blog/profiling-webgpu).
3. **Time frame 1 apart from the build.** Our pipeline-count finding generalizes: the first frame that uses a pipeline/texture/buffer includes creation and compilation cost. Always separate "first frame after load/floor-gen" measurements from steady-state measurements, and report both.
4. **Trace the browser when numbers disagree.** [`chrome://tracing` / Perfetto with the `gpu.dawn` category shows Dawn's actual pipeline-creation, validation, and submission events on their real threads](https://frguthmann.github.io/posts/profiling_webgpu/); the [WebGPU Inspector extension](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/webgpu-inspector/holcbbnljhkpkjkhgkagjkhhpeochfal) plots frame times and live GPU object counts (a direct way to watch pipeline count grow). For shader-level depth on Windows/NVIDIA there are [PIX](https://toji.dev/webgpu-profiling/pix.html) and [Nsight-based flows](https://frguthmann.github.io/posts/profiling_webgpu/).

## 4. Case studies: high-entity-count 2D/2.5D games in the browser

**PixiJS (the workhorse of browser bullet-hells).** PixiJS's entire performance story is batching: [WebGL/WebGPU rendering speed scales roughly with draw calls, and batching many sprites into a single draw call is "the main secret" to its speed](https://pixijs.com/7.x/guides/basics/what-pixijs-is). Its published guidance for high sprite counts reads like an Asteroids design doc: [use spritesheets so batches don't break on texture switches, pool and recycle sprites instead of allocating, avoid per-frame object churn](https://pixijs.com/8.x/guides/concepts/performance-tips). Devlogs document the payoff — [one developer cut 1000+ draw calls to a single draw call and comfortably ran 10,000+ moving sprites](https://rymdkraftverk.itch.io/tails/devlog/83229/pixijs-performance).

**Vampire Survivors (the survivor-like existence proof).** The genre-defining horde game [shipped as an HTML5/JavaScript game on Phaser](https://en.namu.wiki/w/Vampire%20Survivors) — thousands of on-screen enemies and projectiles in a browser-class engine. Community reconstructions and the Phaser ecosystem's own tutorials converge on the same three techniques: [object pooling for enemies/projectiles/particles, spatial partitioning for collision, and strict reuse rather than allocation in the loop](https://github.com/BareTread/vampire-survivors-enhanced) ([Feronato's step-by-step Phaser prototype series](https://emanueleferonato.com/2024/11/29/quick-html5-prototype-of-vampire-survivors-built-with-phaser-like-the-original-game/)). Notably, the developers still [migrated off the HTML5 stack in 2022 explicitly for performance headroom](https://en.namu.wiki/w/Vampire%20Survivors) — a reminder that in the browser, the budget discipline has to be *stronger*, not weaker, than native.

**GC churn is the browser's hidden frame killer.** This matters directly to a TypeScript 60Hz sim: V8's generational collector triggers minor GC ("scavenge") pauses at a rate proportional to allocation rate. Analyses of frame-loop allocation show the arithmetic plainly: [~200 short-lived objects per frame at 60fps is 12,000 allocations/second, filling the young generation every couple of seconds and causing periodic pauses; pooling ~300 pre-allocated objects cut GC pressure by roughly 90% in the cited case study](https://renderlog.in/blog/javascript-garbage-collection-frontend/). The classic [Build New Games guide on GC-friendly code](http://buildnewgames.com/garbage-collector-friendly-code/) codifies the rules: no `new`, no array/closure/string creation in the hot loop; reuse scratch vectors; pool everything with a lifetime shorter than a floor.

**GPU-compute-scale particles.** At the far end, [lisyarus's WebGPU particle-life demo simulates and renders on the order of ten million particles at ~60fps on a mid-range GTX 1060-class GPU](https://lisyarus.github.io/blog/posts/particle-life-simulation-in-browser-using-webgpu.html) — three to four orders of magnitude beyond anything Pinball Knight needs, which is the point: particle count should never again be the reason a frame drops, provided particles live on the GPU.

## 5. Lessons for Pinball Knight

A prioritized, concrete checklist. Priorities: **P0** = do before adding any more content; **P1** = next wave; **P2** = when profiling says so.

### P0 — Pipeline warm-up pass at load (the Asteroids "vector ROM" move)
- During the floor-load screen, assemble a warm-up scene containing **one instance of every material/pipeline the floor can produce**: each zombie/enemy material variant, particle materials (each blend mode), damage-text material, tile/wall/track materials, torch light + shadow variants, fog-of-war overlay, any post/MRT passes — and run [`renderer.compileAsync(warmupScene, camera)`](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/Renderer.html) against the *same render-target configuration the game uses* (see [three.js #31220](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/31220) for why the RT setup matters).
- Add a debug counter for **live pipeline count** (via [WebGPU Inspector](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/webgpu-inspector/holcbbnljhkpkjkhgkagjkhhpeochfal) or a wrapper around device pipeline creation) and treat any pipeline created *after* the warm-up completes as a bug. This is the direct fix for our known ground truth: stalls = pipeline count, first use pays.

### P0 — Material/variant budget (cap it by design, like the 26-asteroid rule)
- Set an explicit budget — a small fixed list of shared materials for the whole game — and enforce it in review: new content reuses an existing material + atlas region, or it justifies a new pipeline in writing. Every "slightly different" material is a new pipeline × every pass it appears in.
- Prefer parameterizing one shader (per-instance color/tint attributes, atlas UV offsets) over cloning materials. Cloned materials with identical shaders can still fragment batching and multiply Three.js render items (see the [UBO-per-render-item cost, three.js #30560](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/30560)).

### P0 — Measurement protocol (before optimizing anything further)
1. Assert real hardware at startup: log [`adapter.info`](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/webgpu/troubleshooting-tips) and refuse to record perf numbers when architecture is `swiftshader` (WSL/headless runs must use host Chrome over CDP, per our WebGPU-target memory).
2. Request `timestamp-query` at device creation when available; wrap the main render pass (and any compute pass) with [GPU timestamps](https://webgpufundamentals.org/webgpu/lessons/webgpu-timing.html); surface pass-time in the debug HUD. Remember Chrome [quantizes the values](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/blink-dev/c/dtYJ0MQYMlU) — read trends, not single samples.
3. Always time **frame 1 apart from the build**: record "first frame after floor generation" and "steady state" as separate metrics, so pipeline/resource-creation cost can't masquerade as (or hide inside) steady-state cost.
4. When JS timings and GPU timestamps disagree, capture a [Perfetto trace with `gpu.dawn`](https://frguthmann.github.io/posts/profiling_webgpu/) rather than guessing.

### P1 — Instanced rendering for the hordes
- **Zombies/enemies:** one `InstancedMesh` (or instanced sprite/billboard) per enemy archetype, per-instance matrix + tint/frame attributes; animation via atlas frame index per instance, not per-object materials. [Single-draw-call rendering of the whole horde](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html) is the difference-maker at our entity counts ([Codrops guide](https://tympanus.net/codrops/2025/07/10/three-js-instances-rendering-multiple-objects-simultaneously/)).
- **Damage numbers:** these are the stealth pipeline/draw-call bomb — implement as one instanced quad batch sampling a pre-baked glyph atlas (consistent with our pixel-text binarized-sprite approach), never as per-number meshes or DOM.
- **Particles:** short term, one instanced quad system per blend mode drawing from a shared atlas. Medium term (P2), move simulation into a WebGPU **compute pass** so gore/sparks scale for free ([compute-particle pattern](https://threejsroadmap.com/blog/galaxy-simulation-webgpu-compute-shaders), [performance rationale](https://markaicode.com/webgpu-physics-simulation-1m-particles/)). Particles must never enter the 60Hz sim or allocate in the tick.
- **Static floor geometry:** at maze-generation time, merge walls/track/rails per material into a few big buffers ([merged BufferGeometry pattern](https://57blocks.com/blog/improve-performance-on-threejs-scenes-using-custom-meshes-and-buffergeometry?tab=engineering)); a generated floor is immutable for its lifetime and should cost a near-constant handful of draws, like a vector-ROM level.

### P1 — Pooling and zero-allocation frame loop (the TypeScript 60Hz sim)
- Pool every short-lived entity: particles, damage numbers, projectiles, hit events, corpses' visual shells. Acquire on spawn, release on death, never `new` inside the tick ([pooling cuts GC pressure dramatically](https://renderlog.in/blog/javascript-garbage-collection-frontend/), [GC-friendly patterns](http://buildnewgames.com/garbage-collector-friendly-code/)).
- Audit the sim tick and render prep for per-frame allocations (spread ops, `.map/.filter`, temp vectors, string building for damage text). Use preallocated scratch objects. A scavenge pause landing inside the accumulator loop causes exactly the "spiral of death" a fixed timestep is vulnerable to.
- This is the same discipline as PixiJS's and the survivor-like community's published practice ([PixiJS performance tips](https://pixijs.com/8.x/guides/concepts/performance-tips), [survivor-like optimization writeups](https://github.com/BareTread/vampire-survivors-enhanced)).

### P1 — Light budget (finish what the torch pool started)
- The torch live-light pool is the right call — keep the hard cap and make it a *named constant with a comment explaining why*, so no future wave "just adds a light." Every additional point light in a forward path multiplies fragment cost and can force new shader permutations (= new pipelines).
- Fake the rest: emissive atlas sprites + additive quads for glow, baked/vertex-tinted ambient per tile from fog-of-war state. Asteroids' best lighting effect (phosphor trails) cost zero cycles; ours should too.

### P2 — Entity caps as game rules
- Adopt the Logg rule: pick explicit maxima (live enemies, live particles, live damage numbers, corpses) and define the over-budget behavior in *design* terms — oldest particle recycled, farthest off-screen zombie despawned, damage numbers coalesced — instead of letting the frame time absorb it. Asteroids shipped "the split just doesn't spawn" and nobody ever filed it as a bug.
- Wraparound's lesson for a maze crawler: keep the *active set* tied to what the player can perceive. Fog of war and floor structure already bound visibility; make the renderer's working set match it (cull by room/corridor, freeze/skip fully-dark regions).

### The one-sentence summary
Asteroids was fast because every frame did the minimum describable work — a short display list over precompiled shapes, hard caps, nothing off-screen — and Pinball Knight gets the same result on WebGPU by pre-compiling every pipeline at load, drawing hordes as instances from atlases, pooling everything with a short lifetime, capping populations by rule, and only ever trusting numbers from a real adapter with GPU timestamps taken one frame apart from the build.
